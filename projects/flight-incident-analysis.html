01 / THE QUESTION

Was this a failed control surface—or a commanded departure?

The aircraft climbed, rolled sharply left and entered a steep descent. Visual replay suggested a control-surface issue, but the dataflash log offered a more reliable route to the initiating cause.

I aligned commanded and measured attitude, received RC inputs, estimated angle of attack, speed, altitude and elevon outputs over the same 64.0–78.2 s interval.

02 / EVENT SEQUENCE

Fourteen seconds from launch to impact.

The channels were read together rather than in isolation. That changed the interpretation from an unexplained roll to an aggressive, recorded command sequence followed by loss of recoverable control authority.

04 / EVIDENCE BOUNDARY

What the log proves—and what it cannot.

A useful incident report must separate measured evidence from plausible explanations. Servo commands are recorded; physical surface position is not.

The log supports

  • A steep pitch-up command during the initial climb.
  • A progressive roll input reaching the recorded full-left limit.
  • Estimated AOA approaching 20° near maximum altitude.
  • Large desired-versus-measured pitch error during recovery.
  • Elevon output saturation before impact.

The log does not prove

  • That a physical elevon moved to the commanded position.
  • That the recorded RC input was deliberate pilot action.
  • That a servo, linkage or power system failed mechanically.
  • That the final extreme AOA value was aerodynamically valid.
  • That stall prevention could make the configured limits safe.

05 / CONFIGURATION

The protection envelope was too permissive for a prototype.

FBWA converts stick position into an attitude demand bounded by the configured limits. The recorded values allowed commands far beyond conservative first-flight settings.

ENGINEERING VERDICT

The left roll was received as a command—not generated independently by the autopilot.

The most probable causal chain was aggressive pitch-up, rising AOA, progressive full-left roll demand, elevon saturation and insufficient height for recovery. A mechanical control-surface problem remains possible, but is not demonstrated by this log.

06 / NEXT FLIGHT

Turn a crash into a better test plan.

The investigation converted an initial assumption into a testable set of corrective actions.

  • Restore conservative roll and pitch limits.
  • Verify the source of the sustained 1000 µs roll input.
  • Test elevon direction, travel and combined-command saturation.
  • Calibrate and configure the airspeed system appropriately.
  • Use greater recovery altitude for the next controlled test.
